# The Illusion of Progress

Rising productivity is usually celebrated as the core engine of human advancement. Creating more value with fewer resources and less time ought to mean higher efficiency, greater prosperity, and more leisure. Yet reality often paints a baffling, even despairing picture: as technology accelerates and productivity grows exponentially, most people do not feel liberated—they feel more exhausted and squeezed than ever. **If gains in productivity only leave human beings more worn out, what is the point of such progress? Are we trapped in an "illusion of progress,"** repeating history's mistakes while refusing to learn from them?

**As Ray Dalio has observed, the essence of a central-bank fiat regime is replacing scarce hard money with endlessly printable paper so that governments cannot go bankrupt on paper—when they cannot pay, they print. Fundamentally, this borrows from the future at the expense of the next generation's purchasing power. Bank runs used to mean redeeming currency for gold; today, a run on currency means fleeing into real goods.** Over the past year, the surge in US equities—especially AI stocks—has been impossible to miss, with AI-linked QQQ holdings climbing nearly 30%. The Trump administration has repeatedly claimed that AI will unleash massive deflation and widespread prosperity. For ordinary people, however, that boom feels eerily unreal:

- Unemployment is climbing—not just in the United States, but across countries;
- Inflation is felt viscerally by everyday citizens almost everywhere, even when headline economic statistics understate it.

This grim paradox is nothing new. History shows that productivity growth and worker well-being are far from linearly correlated. Take the **Great Depression** of the 1930s: American factories had already attained staggering output, churning out automobiles, radios, and consumer goods at unprecedented scale. In pure productivity terms, it was a giant leap forward. Yet that leap coincided with catastrophic economic collapse—overproduction alongside collapsing effective demand, shuttered factories, millions unemployed, and widespread misery. Technological capacity did not translate into shared prosperity or leisure; it produced mass suffering. The lesson is stark: productivity is not an end in itself. Its worth depends on how its fruits are distributed and governed. When the surplus is captured by a tiny minority while the majority face structural unemployment and market instability, "progress" becomes meaningless—or outright destructive—for most people.

As Donald Trump returned to office and reshaped America's relationships with both allies and the wider world, Washington proactively dismantled many assumptions underpinning post-1971 international trade and monetary order. As the global fiat system enters a restructuring cycle, commodities are beginning to re-monetize:

- Gold: `$5,000 / oz`
- SPX / Gold ratio: fallen back to `1.39`
- QQQ / Gold ratio: fallen back to `0.12`

In plain English: priced in US dollars, US stocks are hitting all-time highs; priced in gold, they haven't rallied at all—they are testing lower support levels.

## Through the Lens of Asset Valuation

![M2](https://pbs.twimg.com/media/G_vpmBOaIAAs527?format=jpg&name=medium)

Look at the three lines on this chart:

- **Dashed black line**: The M2-to-gold ratio—the Federal Reserve's money printer. It trends steadily downward, reflecting a 69% depreciation of the US dollar against gold.
- **Solid blue line**: The SPX500-to-gold ratio ("ordinary US equities"), representing the real purchasing power of the S&P 500 priced in gold. It nearly overlaps the dashed black line.
- **Solid orange line**: QQQ / Gold ("tech growth"). It outperforms the black line, yet still sits below its 1999 peak.

Compared with 1999—27 years ago:

- The US dollar has lost **69%** against gold;
- The S&P 500 (SPX) has lost **68%** in gold terms;
- The Nasdaq-100 (QQQ) has lost **30%** in gold terms.

Put another way: **roughly 99% of the S&P 500's nominal gain is purely dollar debasement premium, while 60%–70% of QQQ's gain is also monetary premium—only about 30% represents genuine technological productivity growth.**

Today, gold's rate of appreciation already outpaces both M2 money supply growth and equity gains combined. If the dollar's purchasing power is cut in half again during a fiat restructuring cycle, it will wipe out all real gains delivered by AI.

"Stocks always go up" is an optical illusion. Only returns that beat M2 expansion represent real purchasing power gained—and your assets are losing purchasing power far faster than you think. For context, year-over-year US M2 growth is around 4.6%, while China's is 8.5%. The monetization of commodities is happening in real time. Most investors track nominal returns (in fiat) rather than real purchasing power. In the coming cycle, if your portfolio fails to outrun M2 expansion, you are getting poorer even as your account balance grows. Gold hitting `$5,000 / oz` may not mean gold got expensive—it may mean the denominator (the dollar) was cut in half.

## 1. How Labor Turns into a Relentless Machine Appendage

As mechanized large-scale industry expands, an inevitable trajectory of capitalist value motion emerges: *"objectified labor confronts living labor within the labor process itself as the power that dominates it,"* while living labor is reduced to a *"mere living appendage"* of the machine system. Viewed from value creation, capital's relentless drive for self-valorization inevitably hollows out the substance of value itself—like a black hole in capitalist value circulation that swallows old value without generating new value. As American geographer David Harvey points out: *"If living labor is the source of value and profit, then replacing living labor with dead or mechanical labor makes no sense politically or economically. For Marx, this is a central contradiction of capitalism."*

Looking back at industrial history, under Fordism the capitalist deployment of science and technology drove standardization and automation, inverting the human-machine relationship: *"In handicrafts and manufacture, the worker makes use of a tool; in the factory, the machine makes use of him."* Even then, labor remained indispensable as a "conscious organ." Later, as immaterial labor flourished across daily communication, social cooperation, and affective interaction, capital struggled at first to discipline and absorb it—let alone replace it with an automated machine system. In the era of digital capitalism, intelligent production entered the fast lane, dramatically expanding machine substitution and shrinking direct labor. Yet real-world digital economies reveal that analytical AI remains bounded to narrow tasks, and the hidden proliferation of "ghost work" reminds us that "unmanned," "zero-labor," and "post-work" futures remain techno-optimist utopias.

Even in Marx's framework, when capital achieves its most perfected automatic system of machinery, labor does not vanish completely; rather, it persists as an element *"distributed across many points of the mechanical system."* Marx considered *"a capitalist who employs no variable capital at all—and therefore no workers—in a given branch of production"* an extreme hypothetical limit. From mechanization to automation to digitalization, and from physical labor to immaterial and digital labor, value-creating living labor never truly exited the stage. *"Steam engines, power looms, electrification, and electronics never sustainably reduced total labor—on the contrary, they expanded it."* Yet times change. With the deployment of large language models and general-purpose AI, digital machines are gaining autonomous decision-making and generalized capability at breathtaking speed. Setting aside debates over "superintelligence" or "machine consciousness," from the standpoint of production, today's rapidly iterating "high-order automation" is displacing more and more cognitive work with hyper-efficient algorithms. Whereas earlier industrial automation primarily substituted physical effort and blue-collar workers, generative AI like ChatGPT is upending mental labor and displacing white-collar knowledge workers.

Confronted with the vast power of industrial machinery, Marx once warned: *"If machinery were to eliminate the entire wage-laboring class, that would be a dreadful thing for capital—for without wage labor, capital ceases to be capital!"* Unquestionably, the productive might of LLM-era digital machinery dwarfs every industrial machine before it. As general-purpose AI matures and spreads, capital is reshaping the fundamental faculties of living labor—cognition and perception—into machine-compatible forms, capturing and embedding them into distributed intelligent systems. Follow this trajectory to its conclusion, and an unavoidable question looms: how many opportunities will remain for ordinary workers even to *"stand beside the production process"* tending the machines?

## 2. How LLMs Amplify Capitalism's "Market Paradox" of Value

In political economy, value expresses the social relation through which people exchange labor in a commodity economy. It originates from the contradiction between private and social labor under the division of labor, and materializes through the unity of production and circulation. Mediated by exchange value, concrete labor is converted into abstract labor and private labor into social labor. Affirming labor's social validity through exchange thus becomes a matter of life and death for the capitalist value system.

On the realization side, as productivity surges and commodity supply swells, expanding markets and demand becomes ever more urgent. Yet centuries of capitalist history demonstrate that no matter how aggressively capital pries open domestic and foreign markets, relative overproduction shadows large-scale production at every step. The root cause is an insurmountable **market paradox** baked into capital's own motion: to capture value, capital expands commodity output relentlessly; to accelerate self-valorization, it simultaneously drives down the value of labor power. Expanding production and shrinking purchasing power pull in opposite directions, rupturing value circulation and triggering recurring crises.

This pattern has unfolded in distinct waves: Fordist standardization and assembly lines drove the **deskilling** of labor, reducing workers to appendages performing monotonous, easily learned motions—thereby devaluing their labor power. Under post-Fordism, neoliberal institutional reforms normalized precarious employment, leaving ordinary workers increasingly reliant on consumer credit just to get by. Under digital capitalism, platform organization and algorithmic management deepened employment precarity even further.

Now, general-purpose AI is restructuring workflows across virtually every industry. McKinsey estimates that across 63 use cases, generative AI could unlock `$2.6 trillion` to `$4.4 trillion` in annual economic value globally. Yet today, Big Tech monopolies dominate data, compute, and algorithms, positioning themselves to capture the lion's share of this new digital dividend. Without structural reform of existing platform capitalism, more and more workers will inevitably slide into the ranks of the **"digital poor."**

Two forces drive this shift. First, LLM-powered automation pushes "machine substitution" squarely into knowledge work, meaning the "creative class" is poised to follow blue-collar workers in being cast aside by ruthless, narrative-savvy digital capital. Second, general-purpose AI supercharges **digital Taylorism**: dissecting human cognitive workflows into standardized, modular, automatable micro-tasks, deskilling even high-tier intellectual labor. As complex labor is systematically simplified, labor's share of national income is bound to keep falling.

The result is a two-sided squeeze: on one side, LLMs industrialize knowledge commodities while supercharging traditional manufacturing, pushing total productive capacity upward exponentially; on the other, they displace vast numbers of knowledge workers from direct production, accelerating the hollowing out of the **middle class** that has long anchored mass consumption in developed economies. By magnifying the gap between soaring output and weakening demand, LLM-era capitalism is headed for more frequent overproduction crises and sharper market turbulence.

## 3. A Global Perspective

From a global supply-chain perspective, the United States and China have forged deeply intertwined economic ties. America has long run a massive trade deficit with China, with US corporations relying on Chinese low-cost manufacturing to keep consumer prices low and corporate margins fat. Although some companies have announced reshoring initiatives in recent years, the share of production genuinely returning to US soil remains limited—building factories and recruiting skilled manufacturing labor domestically carries formidable costs.

Against this backdrop, the Trump administration rolled out sweeping tariffs between 2018 and 2020 covering hundreds of billions of dollars in Chinese goods—starting with steel and aluminum before expanding to consumer products—aimed at narrowing the trade deficit, revitalizing American manufacturing, and reshoring supply chains. Reality, however, fell well short of those ambitions.

Meanwhile, China's global position has evolved as well. Climbing up the value chain from the "world's factory," China has pivoted from basic assembly toward higher-value-added industries and technological innovation, projecting greater economic and geopolitical confidence. Notably, Washington's current **"de-risking"** framing differs from full **"decoupling"**—a more calibrated attempt to reduce dependence in critical supply chains without severing economic ties altogether.

It is precisely within this shifting global architecture that the lived reality of Chinese workers exposes the contradictions—and inhumanity—of pure growth logic most starkly.

Over the decades since Reform and Opening-Up, backed by abundant affordable labor and immense organizational capacity, China's productivity exploded, turning it into the world's manufacturing powerhouse and a formidable high-tech contender. Yet right alongside that boom stands a sobering labor reality: entrenched **"996" work schedules**, cutthroat *neijuan* (involution), and grueling hours across both manufacturing and services. Countless young and middle-aged workers are physically and mentally drained, starved of autonomy and leisure; personal quality of life has failed to rise in lockstep with national GDP. Even as aggregate wealth expanded dramatically, vast numbers of workers saw little proportionate reward and instead felt trapped in endless overtime and hyper-competition. Productivity gains enriched owners of capital and technology while leaving ordinary workers ever more overworked.

**The deeper danger is how this dynamic widens wealth inequality. Technological and productive leaps truly unlock enormous economic potential, yet the dividends are distributed wildly unevenly. A small minority controlling capital, technology, and information accumulate wealth effortlessly, while ordinary workers watch their bargaining power erode and wages stagnate under the pressure of automation and AI. In the end, a few enjoy wealth and freedom while the many grind through overtime and anxiety.**

**"*History happens over and over again, people don't learn anything!*"** Fatalistic as it sounds, that exclamation captures a bleak truth. From the Great Depression to today's labor struggles and inequality in China and worldwide, history appears stuck on repeat. Society stays hypnotized by slogans of "efficiency" and "growth," forgetting that the ultimate purpose of progress should be human flourishing. We wield unprecedented productive power, yet we have built neither the social mechanisms nor the ethical norms to distribute it fairly. We still fight over technological dividends by the law of the jungle, forgetting that genuine progress ought to mean less coerced toil, more freedom, and a more equal society.

If rising productivity merely produces harsher exploitation, fiercer competition, wider inequality, and deeper exhaustion for the majority, then it has forfeited its meaning. It is a triumph of engineering, not of human civilization. The measure of progress cannot be GDP growth or output per hour alone; it must be collective well-being, the emancipation of workers, and fairer human relations.

Productivity itself is neutral—its value depends on how humanity chooses to wield it. Can we learn from the Great Depression and from today's labor crisis, shifting our focus away from blindly chasing *"more"* and *"faster"* toward pursuing *"better"* and *"fairer"*? Doing so demands redefining progress, strengthening social safety nets, designing fairer wealth distribution, and crafting human-centered policy. Otherwise, we will remain trapped inside the "illusion of progress," running endlessly toward a glossy yet hollow future. True progress should let everyone share in its abundance—with more leisure and more dignity—rather than whipping us onward without rest. History repeats because humanity keeps chasing profit instead of better lives; we need a fairer society where workers' contributions are respected and rewarded, not exploited.

If rising productivity only makes us work harder, what is it really worth? Only by rethinking our values and institutions—and turning productive breakthroughs into genuine improvements in ordinary people's lives—can we truly enjoy the fruits of technology instead of reliving the mistakes of the past.
