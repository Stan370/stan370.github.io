During World War I, peace briefly broke out in the middle of war.

On Christmas 1914 along the Western Front, despite strict military orders forbidding fraternization with the enemy, British and German soldiers climbed out of their trenches, crossed No Man’s Land, buried their fallen together, traded gifts, and even played games.

Fast-forward to 2024: after decades of relative peace, our capacity to trust one another has somehow eroded dramatically. Surveys over the last forty years show fewer and fewer people saying they trust others. That raises two striking questions:

Why do friends turn into enemies even in peacetime?  
And why can enemies turn into friends even in wartime?

Game theory offers a remarkably clear lens on why distrust spreads like a contagion—and how we can reverse it. If you want to experience the mechanics interactively, try Nicky Case’s brilliant game: https://dccxi.com/trust/

At its core, trust is confidence and reliance grounded in expectations about another person’s reliability, honesty, capability, and intent:

1. **Expectation of behavior**: Built through observation and past experience, letting us anticipate that others will act consistently with shared values.
2. **Faith in competence and integrity**: Believing someone both *can* follow through and *will* act ethically.
3. **Vulnerability under uncertainty**: Trust only exists where risk exists—placing something valuable in another’s hands.
4. **Reciprocity**: Trust is two-way and sustained through mutual transparency and respect.

In repeated games, a simple **Copycat** (Tit-for-Tat—start by cooperating, then mirror what the other player did last) thrives alongside cooperative peers and punishes **Cheaters** who try to exploit it. “Do unto others” turns out to be not just a moral maxim, but a mathematical equilibrium—*with one critical catch*.

If you don’t play enough rounds (say, five or fewer), Cheaters dominate completely.

In 1985, when Americans were asked how many close friends they had, the most common answer was **three**. By 2014, the most common answer was **zero**. Fewer close friendships mean fewer **repeated interactions** across communities—and fewer repeated interactions cause distrust to spread rapidly. (Mass media is one-way; it does not count as repeated two-way interaction.)

Something similar happens when the payoff for **mutual cooperation** shrinks: Cheaters gain the upper hand. Game theory contrasts two worlds:

- **Zero-sum games**: One side’s gain must come directly from the other’s loss.
- **Non-zero-sum games**: Both sides can create win-win outcomes (or jointly avoid lose-lose ones). Without non-zero-sum incentives, trust cannot evolve.

Game theory distills three conditions required for trust to grow:

1. **Repeated interactions**: You need to know you will meet again.
2. **Possible win-win outcomes**: The game must be non-zero-sum.
3. **Low miscommunication**: Too many misunderstandings shred trust—while occasional minor mistakes actually give forgiveness room to emerge.

**Today’s crisis isn’t simply that people are losing trust—it’s that our environment violates the conditions under which trust evolves.**

“We are merely products of our environment” sounds cynical, yet game theory reminds us that *we are each other’s environment*. In the short run, the game defines the players; in the long run, the players define the game.

### Credibility and How We Handle Mistakes

1. **Own your mistakes**: Acknowledging errors and taking responsibility signals honesty and maturity.
2. **Learn and adapt**: Turning failures into concrete improvements earns far more trust than pretending to be infallible.
3. **Honesty and transparency**: Clear, candid communication when things go wrong preserves credibility.
4. **One mistake isn’t the whole story**: What matters most is willingness to grow.

### Traits That Earn Trust

- **Consistency**: Reliable character and follow-through over time.
- **Integrity**: Straightforward honesty, especially when it’s uncomfortable.
- **Accountability**: Owning outcomes instead of shifting blame.
- **Growth**: Demonstrating genuine learning from setbacks.

Everyone makes mistakes. At a 0% error rate, strict Copycat wins; between 1% and 9%, **Forgiving Copycat** wins; between 10% and 49%, ruthless Cheaters win; at 50%, **nobody wins consistently**.

That is why miscommunication is such a fascinating barrier to trust: a tiny rate of error rewards forgiveness, while chronic misunderstanding triggers cascading distrust. Modern communication tools connect us more frequently than ever, yet stripping away context often multiplies misunderstandings.

Across East Asian cultures, failure has historically carried heavy stigma. That norm is gradually shifting as more people recognize failure as an unavoidable part of learning. Founders, engineers, and teammates who have weathered setbacks honestly—and grown from them—often turn out to be the most resilient and trustworthy people you can work with.
