“That day I turned twenty-one, in the golden age of my life. I had so many wild wishes: I wanted to love, to eat, and to transform in an instant into a half-bright, half-shadowed cloud in the sky. Only later did I learn that life is a slow process of getting hammered down—day by day you grow older, your wishes fade away, and in the end you turn into a beaten ox. Yet on my twenty-first birthday I foresaw none of this. I believed I would stay fierce forever, and nothing could ever hammer me down.” — Wang Xiaobo

After graduating from university, I have spent a semester and a half at home seemingly “doing nothing.” Relatives and friends keep checking in on me, their words carrying a quiet undertone of anxiety: “Hurry up and find a job.” Under that collective gaze, not jumping straight into the workforce makes me feel like an outlier. Society usually expects young people to follow a single, pre-approved script: finish school, land a job, settle down. Yet everyone’s pace and sense of value are unique; I don’t think we should rush into major decisions merely because of outside noise. To me, making money is a means rather than an end—growing into a whole person and listening to your inner voice can be far more meaningful than bending to external expectations.

What year was age twenty-three for you? For me, it is right now—and how should I meet this stretch of my life? It is March 2024. Around this time last year, I had just received offers from UofT and TAMU, wrapping up an exhausting admissions season. I wasn’t ecstatic, just quietly relieved that some of the uncertainty ahead seemed to have lifted. Things didn’t go smoothly, though: my plans stalled out in an indefinite IRCC security background check, falling apart just steps from the finish line. Without Proclamation 10043, or if geopolitics were even slightly warmer, would I be studying abroad right now, following my original plan and confronting an entirely different set of challenges? Sadly, there are no “what ifs”—only reality.

In this stretch of quiet reflection, I’ve slowly realized that moving a little slower isn’t necessarily a misfortune. This time has given me space to look inward and ask what truly matters to me. Perhaps this period that looks like “doing nothing” from the outside is actually a rare chance to lay a firmer foundation for what comes next. After all, life is full of unknowns and detours; learning to anchor myself in the middle of uncertainty may be the most valuable lesson of this chapter.

Wishing strength to all of us.
